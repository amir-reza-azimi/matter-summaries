Frontier capability and how to test it. Andrej Karpathy posted the most substantive thing in the window: a note on how we're outgrowing simple capability tests like "draw an SVG of a pelican on a bicycle." As a next-generation test, he gave Opus 5 the opening paragraph of Lord of the Rings, a million-token budget worth about ten dollars, and asked for a three.js render of it. Opus worked for roughly two hours and produced fifty five hundred lines of code that procedurally rendered the scene, placing and animating polygon assets in three-dimensional space entirely from a paragraph of prose. He calls this the shift from "no one would ever spend the time to build something this custom" to "sure, why not, it's basically free," and imagines a future of hyper-custom, on-demand worlds, an ephemeral game built around any story or idea you name. His sharpest observation is about the limitation this exposes: the model cannot natively watch its own output. Opus had to painstakingly take screenshots at intervals to check its work, and still produced visible jank, because current models are still weak at native video and gameplay perception, so they cannot cheaply audit what they build in a visual, playable medium the way they can audit text or code.

What an elite, fully-specified prompt actually looks like. Jason Liu, now doing developer-experience and marketing work at OpenAI, posted the full prompt he uses to one-shot a complete, polished browser game in a single HTML file: a downhill snowboarding runner built on three.js, with no build step and no external assets. What makes it worth flagging isn't the game itself, it's the sheer density of specification: exact hex colors and lighting angles, a seeded pseudo-random number generator so terrain is reproducible, precise physics constants for edge grip and drag, a full post-processing pipeline named pass by pass, and even a note clarifying which visual assets the model should substitute when the exact ones aren't available. It is a genuinely useful reference for how much detail separates a mediocre one-shot from a shippable one, and it is worth actually opening if you want to see the full structure, because a paraphrase understates how exhaustive it is. For teaching non-technical people how to prompt for complex builds, this is close to a template: name every constraint you'd otherwise leave implicit, and the model stops guessing.

Where AI-native product design is heading. Ammaar Reshi shared an update on Google AI Studio's mobile app, revealing that it has already logged over eight hundred thousand pre-orders across a hundred and sixty eight countries. Rather than shipping another standalone app, the team's bet is that apps should emerge naturally out of an ongoing conversation with Gemini itself, on both mobile and desktop, while the AI Studio web experience keeps serving people who want to go from idea to prompt to an actual business. It's a concrete signal of where a major lab thinks personal software creation is heading: less app store, more conversation that turns into software on demand. Separately, Logan Kilpatrick posted a real, usable shipped update to Google's Gemini Managed Agents API: support for model environment hooks like linting and blocking, the ability to pin a specific model version, free-tier access in both the UI and the API, and an updated default agent now running on Gemini 3.6 Flash. If you're evaluating managed-agent frameworks as a reference point for BI or marketing tooling, this is the kind of concrete capability delta worth tracking even outside the Claude ecosystem.

Nothing from Boris Cherny or Jason Liu's RAG-and-consulting era this week is worth flagging on its own; Jason's timeline has visibly shifted toward promoting ChatGPT and Codex as an OpenAI employee rather than independent AI-consulting content, which is worth knowing simply so you calibrate how much signal to expect from that account going forward.

Why this matters for my work. The Karpathy and Jason Liu posts are really two sides of the same coin: as models get more capable, the bottleneck shifts from what the model can do to how precisely you can specify what you want, and how well you can verify what it actually produced. That is directly relevant to the BI knowledge layer and any self-serve analytics agent I build at join.com, where the model's output needs the same kind of exhaustive, unambiguous spec that Jason wrote for a snowboarding game, and the same kind of self-auditing gap Karpathy points at, a text-and-code model that cannot cheaply check a dashboard or chart the way it checks its own code, is worth designing around from day one. The Gemini Managed Agents update is a useful comparison point for whatever managed-agent framework choices come up for BI or marketing tooling, even outside the Claude ecosystem, and Ammaar's AI Studio note is a small but real data point for the AI-native consulting material: a major lab is now betting that software creation collapses into conversation rather than app installs, which is exactly the framing worth testing on clients going AI-native.
