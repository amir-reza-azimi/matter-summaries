18 Months of Pricing AI Automations in 21 Mins

Channel: Nate Herk | AI Automation · Published: 2026-08-03 · Length: 21 min · Watch on YouTube

Nate opens by admitting he priced a lot of his first hundred-plus AI automation builds wrong: undercharged, underscoped, and unable to explain a number when a client pushed back on it. This video is everything he now knows about pricing AI work, walked through against one real build he sold, with every number shown.

The example is an appointment-setting agent. Employees were manually setting about twenty meetings a week, each taking roughly an hour, at about forty dollars an hour fully loaded, which comes out to eight hundred dollars a week or forty one thousand six hundred dollars a year. Before naming any price, Nate walked the client through the full solution and ran a discovery phase to understand the actual complexity, because you cannot ballpark a number without understanding how deeply the system needs to integrate. He then priced the build at roughly thirteen percent of that annualized number, five thousand five hundred dollars, which put the client at about a seven and a half times return in year one. His rule of thumb is to aim for a client seeing a ten times return, because that math makes the deal very hard to say no to. On top of the build he added a four hundred dollar a month maintenance retainer, and he is precise about what that covers: it guarantees the system keeps doing what was agreed, covering breakage, API changes, or model updates, not new functionality, which is always a separate conversation. His one regret on this project was not going back afterward to measure the actual before-and-after transformation, which cost him leverage in the next sales conversation. Capture the baseline up front, then follow up at one, two, and three months to prove the number moved.

He then makes the case against hourly billing, crediting pricing consultant Jonathan Stark for the framing. Hourly billing pays you more for being slow: bill two people the same rate and the faster one earns you less for delivering more value, so getting good at your job literally cuts your income. As AI tools compress a week of work into an afternoon, hourly pricing directly punishes that speed. Hourly is fine for your first two or three projects when you have no proof points yet, but after that, stop. Instead, price against three words: cost, value, and price. Cost is your floor, value is the ceiling of what the outcome is worth to the client, and price is any number between the two. Jonathan's landscaper example makes the point sharply: a landscaper cannot raise the price of the same lawn because his own costs went up. Cost does not justify price. Price justifies cost.

Finding the client's ceiling takes one conversation. Let them dump everything first, what they have tried, what worked, where it breaks, then ask what changes for the business once this is running. Then run three buckets of questions: why this, why now, and why me. Why this checks whether what they are asking for actually solves their problem, since clients often ask for an agent when the real fix is a simple deterministic script and a Slack alert. Why now surfaces real urgency, a closing window or competitive pressure, which is real money. Why me is the uncomfortable one: literally ask why they would not just build this internally, hand it to an intern, or vibe code it themselves. That question surfaces objections that are already in their head, so you can answer them on the call instead of losing the deal later. If a client keeps pushing for a number before you can ask these questions, or clearly just wants a quote to shop around, that is a signal to walk away, because they are optimizing for cheapest labor, not a partner.

When clients will not hand over hard numbers, three simpler sizing questions work: how long does this take today, how many people touch it, and what happens when it breaks. From the resulting annualized value, Nate anchors his starting price between ten and twenty percent of that number, and always presents three tiered packages rather than one price, because one number makes the decision binary while three numbers make the decision about which tier to pick, which is a much easier yes. Milestones matter as much as the price itself: split payment into objective, provable checkpoints, like a working proof of concept that answers a question from the database within a minute, never a subjective one like the agent working as expected, which invites endless argument and scope creep. When a client pushes back on price, the fix is to shrink scope, not silently drop the number, so you never train them that frowning moves your price down. On costs, he is firm that API and token usage always run on the client's own account. His fee is for design, consulting, building, and testing; token usage is a utility bill and utility bills belong in the client's name, something he learned the hard way after months of babysitting reimbursements under his own billing.

The single most useful move he leaves you with: before you say any number, get the client to state out loud, in their own words, exactly what the problem is costing them. Once that number is on the table in their language, your price is just a fraction of a number they already said themselves.
