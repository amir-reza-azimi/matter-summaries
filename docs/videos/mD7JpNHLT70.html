Marketing Agents Masterclass (GROW your startup)

Channel: Greg Isenberg · Published: 2026-08-05 · Length: 44 min · Watch on YouTube

This is a practical look at two marketing agents that are useful precisely because they are not presented as autonomous magic. Both are data workflows with a few specific points where an LLM adds judgement. The first finds people already signalling interest in a topic and turns that signal into a verified outbound list. The second turns the expertise already circulating in a team into a measurable LinkedIn content operation. In both cases, the agent is only one part of a system that also includes source data, rules, infrastructure, review, and a feedback loop.

Signal-led outbound, not a larger list

The outbound workflow starts with a different view of targeting. Instead of purchasing a static list or relying only on title and company filters, it watches people who engage with a small group of relevant creators and company accounts. A like or comment is treated as a current hand raise. The recommendation is to choose roughly ten to twenty accounts that capture the audience a business wants to reach. That does not produce complete market coverage, but it produces a high-signal slice of it without paying for every possible contact.

Those engagements are collected through a scraping layer such as Apify and LinkedIn actors. A script then takes a post URL, extracts the people who reacted or commented, and removes duplicates. This is a useful boundary between automation and agency: collecting and normalising records is deterministic work, while the agent can help decide whether a creator, post, or engagement signal is actually relevant to a campaign.

The enrichment waterfall and its guardrails

The collected LinkedIn URLs enter a waterfall, moving from lower-cost providers to higher-cost ones. The example begins with GitLeads, then tries Apollo, then uses Origami or Prospeo for the remaining contacts. LeadMagic can add mobile numbers. The important detail is the ordering. A waterfall makes the cost of each recovered contact visible instead of treating enrichment as one expensive black box.

Every recovered email then goes through Million Verifier. That matters because an agentic outbound process fails if it only optimises for volume. Invalid addresses damage deliverability and can contaminate the main company domain. The demonstration gives a concrete conversion shape: from fifty LinkedIn profiles, thirty-two emails arrive in the first enrichment step, ten more in the second, and the remainder continue down the waterfall. This turns a vague automation into a process with measurable yields and clear intervention points.

Sending infrastructure is kept in separate lanes. Cold outreach uses burner domains and dedicated inboxes, while marketing email, transactional messages, and the company's core email stay separate. The operating cost cited is around $100 for sending infrastructure that can support about ten thousand cold emails, plus the sending platform. The real lesson is not the exact vendor stack. It is to design deliverability, verification, and domain isolation before letting a volume-generating agent run.

The organic LinkedIn engine

The second workflow begins with real human material: sales calls, internal conversations, meeting transcripts, and weekly interviews with team members. Rather than asking an LLM to invent thought leadership from a blank page, the system extracts claims, examples, and language from work the company actually did. It drafts posts in the relevant person's voice and schedules them to their own LinkedIn account.

Performance data closes the loop. The workflow observes which ideas perform, remixes the winners, and revisits strong themes on a roughly ninety-day cycle. This is closer to a good social-media manager's operating model than to automated content spam. The agent helps preserve a cadence and find reuse opportunities, but the source material and feedback data determine what deserves repetition.

Why this matters for my work

For JOIN marketing, this is a useful reference architecture for combining intent signals, enrichment controls, and expert-led content. For the 10x Operator, it is a teachable example of what an AI-native commercial process looks like when it has defined inputs, explicit costs, separate risk controls, and a feedback loop. I could map one existing process into the same sequence, test it manually on a small set, and automate only the stable parts.

What separates the system from outreach theatre

The strongest part of the example is that every handoff has an observable output. The creator list gives a defined market lens. Engagement collection produces a lead pool. The enrichment waterfall produces a recoverable-contact rate. Verification produces a deliverability signal. And the content loop produces performance data. That is what makes this an operating system rather than a collection of AI tools. If any stage is weak, the next stage makes the weakness visible.

For a first test, the sensible move is not to reproduce the whole stack. Pick one narrow audience, ten source accounts, and a small number of recent posts. Measure how many people are relevant, how many can be enriched, how many emails verify, and whether the resulting messages lead to real conversations. Only then is it worth adding more providers, volumes, or automation. The decision is ultimately commercial: a system that increases list size but does not improve qualified conversations is merely producing activity.

The strategic point

There is a shared design principle between the outbound and content examples. Both begin with genuine signal rather than a model inventing demand. Engagement is a signal of attention. Calls, meetings, and internal expertise are signals of something worth saying. The agent helps capture, organise, and reuse those signals. It should not be asked to fabricate the source material or the judgement about what a market needs.
