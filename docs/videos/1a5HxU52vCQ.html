Masterclass: How FDEs make $1M/yr deploying AI agents

Channel: Greg Isenberg · Published: 2026-10-01T20:30:04+00:00 · Length: 52.0 min · Watch on YouTube

TL;DR

This is the strongest video in the batch because it replaces “apply AI” with a repeatable delivery method. The guest’s central claim is that AI transformation begins with process discovery and re-engineering, not model selection. A forward-deployed engineer earns trust by mapping how work really moves, deciding which steps should disappear, become deterministic software, become agent work, or remain human decisions, then deploying inside the systems people already use.

Walkthrough

The video frames the commercial opportunity through AI-enabled service firms and roll-ups, but the useful content is the operating playbook. Existing businesses contain fragmented systems, undocumented exceptions, and knowledge held by long-tenured employees. A model cannot safely solve that merely by being connected to a drive. The starting point is a bounded department and a process map built from interviews, available documentation, system data, and observed hand-offs.

The first phase is discovery. Interview the people who own accounts payable, receivables, reconciliation, billing, banking, FP&A, or the equivalent functions in the target team. Ask what starts the work, which system receives it, what transformations happen, where it waits, what needs approval, and which exceptions force a person to intervene. This creates a “human API” from information that does not exist in clean documentation. The transcript is clear that system names are the easy part; the real implementation risk lies in regional differences, acquired companies, historical workarounds, and the hidden rules people apply every day.

Then re-engineer rather than automate every existing step. The proposed four-way sort is practical: delete work that has no current value; use normal deterministic code for stable operations; use an agent where language, judgment, or variable inputs are genuinely needed; and retain human review for consequential exceptions. That categorization is a useful antidote to both over-automation and expensive agent theatre. It also gives stakeholders a visible explanation of why each component exists.

Deployment should happen within systems of record wherever feasible. A workflow that asks staff to use a separate AI portal usually creates new hand-offs and weakens adoption. Instead, agents prepare work in the existing CRM, ERP, inbox, or ticketing system and route exceptions to accountable humans. The video uses accounts payable as an example: map intake, matching, coding, exceptions, approval, and payment; identify cycle-time bottlenecks; automate the predictable parts; and retain review when a document, vendor, or amount falls outside policy.

The last step is commercial and operational measurement. Sell an outcome tied to the buyer’s problem, not a vague promise of intelligence. Baseline cycle time, error rate, manual touches, cost per invoice, revenue leakage, or another relevant KPI. Start narrow enough to prove the workflow, document corrections, and turn recurring corrections into the next version of the operating rules. The resulting correction log becomes a defensible asset because it captures how the business actually decides.

Standout moments

The useful distinction is between knowing a system and knowing the actual process. Another standout is the four-way step classification, which gives a client a concrete workshop format. Finally, the insistence on building into systems of record makes deployment and adoption part of the technical design rather than an afterthought.
