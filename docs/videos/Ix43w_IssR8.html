Screensharing How to Start an AI Agent Business Today

Channel: Greg Isenberg · Published: 2026-07-02 · Length: 30 min · Watch on YouTube

Rather than another billion-dollar startup pitch, this is a live, screen-recorded build of small, deliberately boring AI-agent business ideas using GenSpark Claw — described as a cloud-based, more secure alternative to OpenClaw with built-in skills — set up entirely through a Slack-connected assistant in about four minutes.

The first idea demoed is a "dead domain flipper": the assistant is given a one-line brief (monitor expired domain drops and GoDaddy auctions against criteria like domain-rating 20+, a clean backlink profile, no adult/gambling history, budget under $200) and it responds by asking clarifying questions before building — niche keywords, delivery channel, whether to integrate a domain-authority checker — then delivers a daily ranked shortlist of ten domains worth bidding on, posted into a dedicated Slack channel. The stated business model, drawn from the creator's own past experience running a premium domain marketplace, is buying flagged domains cheap, pairing them with a quick logo, and reselling the bundle for a few thousand dollars.

The second idea is a local-liquidation arbitrage finder: the assistant is pointed at Craigslist, BidSpotter, and local bankruptcy-court listings for a specific city, told to detect restaurant-equipment listings, extract item type and price from unstructured text, build a comparison database against roughly 40 equipment types using live eBay sold-price lookups, and surface a "deal card" showing the spread between asking price and established resale value for a 15-30% broker fee — no inventory risk required, since you can either buy-and-flip or simply broker the introduction. A live run flagged a $1,500 kitchen hood being sold for a small fraction of that value, illustrating the arbitrage the tool is built to surface (restaurant owners in the middle of a liquidation typically don't have time to price or market items well).

A third idea is built live on camera end to end: monitoring job boards (Hacker News "who's hiring", Remotive, Greenhouse) for hiring signals that indicate budget is being spent — a company hiring a head of growth, for instance, signals a marketing-services opportunity — then enriching the company, finding a decision-maker's LinkedIn, and drafting outreach that references the exact job post. Built from a single one-line prompt, the assistant asks a short series of clarifying questions (what's being sold, which signals matter most) before scraping roughly 222 listings, surfacing 14 qualifying companies, and producing a personalized cold-email draft for each — including catching and fixing a formatting bug (stray HTML entities from the scraped job description) simply by describing the problem in plain language rather than debugging code directly.

The episode then generalizes into a reusable sourcing framework for this whole category of idea: look for a messy public feed (marketplaces, listings, auction sites, closures, job postings), a mispriced or neglected asset (a domain, an app, equipment, a small SaaS), a trigger event (a drop, a shutdown, a hiring signal, a rank decline), and an obvious buyer with money (an operator, an agency, a founder, a new owner) — then decide the monetization mechanism (flip, broker, retainer, relaunch). Three more idea sketches are given in the same shape: scanning business-for-sale marketplaces to generate a should-I-buy-this memo in minutes; finding dead Product Hunt launches whose SEO traffic is still alive so you can acquire the domain cheaply from a founder happy to stop paying hosting; and monitoring competitor pricing, job postings, and public changelogs overnight to deliver a one-page "what moved while you slept" competitive-intelligence brief, sellable as a subscription service.

The framing throughout is that these ideas represent a move from selling software per seat to selling an agent tied to an outcome — a distinction increasingly summarized as "agents are the new SaaS."

The concrete, repeatable sourcing framework (feed → asset → trigger → buyer → monetization) is the most directly reusable piece for amir-azimi client work or course material — it's a compact mental model for spotting AI-agent business opportunities that doesn't require the specific tools shown to apply.
