The $1M+ Solo AI Agent Business (Full Course)

Channel: Greg Isenberg · Published: 2026-07-09 · Length: 48 min · Watch on YouTube

Nick from Orgo returns to break down, step by step, how to run a solo AI-agent business selling what he frames as 'digital employees' rather than access to a chatbot — a business he says can be started by one person and scaled to seven figures. The core insight up front: if you can already set up Claude Code, Hermes agent, or OpenClaw, that skill is more valuable than you probably think, because most businesses have no time and no internal capability to do it themselves.

The offer itself is deliberately abstracted away from technical detail. Customers don't want to think about tokens, models, infrastructure, or what breaks when something breaks — they just want it to work. The recommended structure is unlimited agents, unlimited usage, unlimited support and monitoring, priced around $5,000 a month. The trick that makes that math work: customers think they need far more agents than they actually do. Most businesses genuinely need one, two, or maybe three well-built agents, not an unlimited fleet — so pricing as unlimited removes the friction of a metered conversation while your actual token costs stay controlled by how few agents you really need to build well. The pitch language matters too: talk about outcomes and business value, not "time saved," which the video argues customers have become numb to.

On which industries to target, healthcare and finance are explicitly ruled out early — too much regulatory burden for a solo operator. The recommended verticals instead are marketing agencies, law firms, insurance agencies, manufacturers, wholesalers, and real estate — relatively legacy industries that want to become AI-native but don't know how, and that are large enough categories to require picking a sub-niche (a specific region, a specific type of practice within law or real estate) to build an irresistible, specific-feeling offer. The common pattern across every one of these buyer types, regardless of industry, is the same set of executive pain points: too many emails, too many meetings, too many open loops, and context scattered across too many places and people — meaning a lot of the agent's core capability can be templated before you even layer in vertical-specific skills (case management for a law firm, demand letters for a matrimonial practice, and so on).

Getting customers is treated as inseparable from the technical build: making content consistently is described as the single most leveraged activity in 2026, both because it puts your offer in front of buyers and because it builds the kind of warm, pre-informed relationship that makes a sales call close far faster than any cold outreach.

The tool stack gets specific. Granola records every meeting and syncs notes automatically into a Trello board that functions as a customer-facing project management system, with backlog/to-do/doing/done columns — deliberately used to contain scope creep by limiting new agent requests to one or two per 48-hour window. Loom sends customers regular video updates on what's been improved. For the agent itself: Hermes agent is the recommended harness over raw Claude Code or Codex, chosen specifically because it lets you swap the underlying model freely as new, cheaper, more capable models ship, rather than marrying the business to one provider's roadmap. Orgo (the co-founder's own company) gives each agent a persistent cloud computer to live in — the reasoning being that agents managing a business need to be reachable and operable from anywhere, not tied to a physical Mac Mini that would require in-person debugging if something broke at the hardware level, and that isolated cloud computers can be destroyed and recreated in seconds if something goes wrong, limiting blast radius. On top of that, every agent gets Composio (a single connector giving access to thousands of app integrations, plus handling tool-calling and authentication — described as the single biggest time-sink otherwise), Agent Mail (giving each agent its own email address for a more personal, assistant-like feel), and an Obsidian vault as the agent's context and memory layer, described as functioning as a genuine second brain once well organized.

Reliability practices matter as much as the initial build: setting up a watchdog that automatically restarts a crashed gateway (the connection layer to Telegram, WhatsApp, etc.) is treated as essential since these crash periodically, and having the agent itself send an email alert when a scheduled task or skill fails means you find out before the customer does.

For amir-azimi, this is close to a direct blueprint for positioning an AI-agent consulting offer — the unlimited-usage framing, vertical selection logic, and reliability-layer checklist (watchdog, failure alerts) are all concrete enough to adapt into a service offering or into course material on how a solo operator can package and price AI agent work for legacy commercial teams.
