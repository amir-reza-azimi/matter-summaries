FDE: The $1M/Year AI Job Explained

Channel: Greg Isenberg · Published: 2026-07-21 · Length: 52 min · Watch on YouTube

TL;DR: Voss, who runs Veric Agents, gives the clearest breakdown around of what a forward-deployed engineer (FDE) actually is: the person who bridges a business's messy real-world reality and generic AI intelligence, and who is now one of the best-paid roles in tech, from a $150,000 base plus equity up to seven figures for the best. His core thesis: every company can now buy the same intelligence, so the model is no longer the advantage, the advantage is entirely in how, where, and why that intelligence gets deployed inside one specific company.

Why the role exists now. A new frontier model ships almost weekly, and any company can buy access to the same one. Talk to fifty different enterprise clients, Voss says, and they're all running the same stack, Claude, Codex, Cursor, GitHub Copilot. Since intelligence itself is commoditized, competitive advantage moves entirely to deployment: who can bridge the gap between a company's actual, specific processes and that generic intelligence. Palantir popularized the term for exactly this kind of embedded, on-site consulting-for-software role, and Voss argues the AI age is going to demand this ten times more than the data age did, because every company will need custom agents built around its own workflows.

Stage one: understand the business reality. This is where Voss says the bulk of the time goes, and it's the part that gets glossed over by people who are purely technical. The documented process is almost never the real process. His example: 'an email arrives' sounds like a clean trigger, but in reality it arrives from 40-plus senders, none formatted alike, some data in a PDF, some in a screenshot, some in a spreadsheet, or buried in a forwarded thread, and half the cases are exceptions that only exist in one person's head, never written down anywhere. FDEs go on-site (or at minimum, embed deeply and remotely) specifically because sitting with someone for a full eight-to-ten-hour day surfaces the undocumented exceptions that a one-hour interview never will, the same reason McKenzie consultants will literally go sit with miners at a mine rather than just interview a manager about the job.

Stage two: FD judgment, deciding where intelligence belongs and where it doesn't. Voss is blunt about the early-AI-wave mistake: 'let's just slap AI everywhere, let's give everything to the model,' which produced token-maxing and hallucinations, and is part of why the MIT stat that 95% of generative AI pilots fail keeps getting cited. The fix is selectivity: of a ten-step workflow, maybe only three steps genuinely need non-deterministic judgment (something like categorizing an ambiguous lead), while the rest is better served by deterministic if-then logic or plain API calls. Deciding that split, and being confident enough in the technical trade-offs to say 'this design will only hit 80% accuracy and isn't worth the risk' versus 'this one is low-risk and high-ROI,' is where the FDE earns their money. Voss shares a cautionary anecdote from a C-suite executive who blew through a ten-million-dollar annual Claude budget in three months by handing token access to everyone company-wide with no FDE discipline behind it, and the spend simply didn't move the business.

Stage three: build, then run a proper audit-eval-deploy loop. Once judgment calls are made, the FDE's job splits into auditing the workflow, building evaluation suites to prove the system behaves correctly, and finally deploying with a human-in-the-loop approval step before anything goes live end to end. For clearly measurable tasks (was this email categorized correctly against 10,000 historical examples), evals are straightforward. For fuzzier creative output like a generated presentation, Voss's approach is to build a 'golden dataset' from however much historical data exists (past decks, in this case) to define what good actually looks like, codifying preferences like logo placement and font sizing, while accepting evals alone will never be perfect and a standing human-feedback loop has to keep improving the system continuously.

On tooling, be a specialist first, generalist second. Asked whether FDEs should be model-agnostic, Voss's own company positions itself that way commercially, but his direct advice for anyone becoming an FDE is the opposite when starting out: pick one model and one agent-building platform (Claude's agent SDK, OpenAI's equivalent, whichever) and get genuinely excellent at it first. Model-agnosticism is a value proposition for the business you eventually run, not a starting skill; depth in one stack builds the judgment that transfers.

Why this matters for my work: this maps almost exactly onto the bridge Amir is already building between BI/data and AI-native transformation at join.com, and onto Ranger AI's actual client work. The three-stage framework (deeply understand the undocumented reality first, then explicitly decide deterministic-vs-agent-vs-human-loop for each step, then audit/eval/deploy) is directly usable as a structured methodology to bring into consulting engagements and the course, and the 95%-of-pilots-fail / token-maxing cautionary framing is a sharp, credible talking point for why disciplined FDE-style deployment beats simply handing teams AI access.
