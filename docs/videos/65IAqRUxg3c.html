AI Agents run my business and life (Andrew Wilkinson)

Channel: Greg Isenberg · Published: 2026-06-28 · Length: 47 min · Watch on YouTube

Andrew Wilkinson (Tiny, Metalab) walks through a genuinely lived-in AI-agent setup rather than a demo built for the camera, starting from a specific moment: traveling without his laptop, running his entire business from an OpenClaw agent in the back of Ubers, with nobody noticing every email he sent that day had been written by the agent.

His first real product built this way is Deep Personality — a 40-minute multiple-choice psychological assessment (drawing on established clinical screens) that produces a genuinely long, Robert Greene-style narrative report covering archetypes, relationship patterns, attachment style, and whether you show traits of ADHD, OCD, or fall on the spectrum. He built the entire product himself specifically to avoid the friction of coordinating a team through a vision he alone holds clearly — the same instinct that made him choose vibe coding over calling in his own design agency, Metalab. The business itself now runs almost entirely on agents: a support agent handles tickets directly or escalates to a dev agent (which auto-merges a fix immediately for anything flagged as a severe security issue), and a marketing agent connected to PostHog, Meta, and Reddit runs multivariate ad tests, writes creative, and adjusts budget on command — at roughly $20,000 in revenue so far, with Wilkinson genuinely curious what happens once that marketing agent is handed a $100,000/month ad budget.

The harness running all of this shifted from OpenClaw to a tool called Harbor (built by a friend, open-sourced on GitHub), specifically because OpenClaw's text-only interface made it hard to track what multiple agents were actually doing simultaneously — Harbor gives each agent (dev, marketing, support) its own visible lane, closer to an org chart than a chat log. His honest assessment of the current ceiling: today's agents behave like a highly capable but completely inexperienced new hire — genuinely intelligent, but requiring you to specify every operating norm explicitly (check email this often, respond this way) rather than exercising independent judgment, which is why he's skeptical of companies currently selling "fully autonomous businesses" as a real product today, even while expecting the gap to close within the next year or two as context windows keep expanding.

Beyond the personality-test business, the more transferable material is how he's built personal and organizational memory. Fireflies records every meeting; a nightly cron job converts each into a markdown file and files it into GBrain, a friend's vector-database tool for personal knowledge management. He's built separate vector databases for his family office (Foley Partners) and for Tiny's portfolio of roughly 24 companies, letting him ask plain questions — how many minority venture investments have I made, and how many are in the money versus written off — and get an answer pulled directly from years of historical data that would otherwise be too much for one person to hold in their head, functioning, in his words, as "the eye of Sauron" inside a company with too much going on to track manually.

The most concrete, directly reusable moment is a prompting technique: rather than trying to write the perfect prompt yourself, tell the agent "ask me a shitload of questions to determine your prompt, and use the question tool" — letting the model interview you (often for five to ten minutes) to construct a far more complete brief than you'd likely write unprompted, on the reasoning that models are now fully capable of doing this and the perceived need for humans to "build prompts" as a durable skill is largely gone. He pairs this with a standing instruction to always use a team of roughly eight sub-agents for any non-trivial task, reporting that the answers come back meaningfully better.

Two other build examples stand out: his CFO, with zero prior coding background, built a full internal financial dashboard in about two weeks to replace a $50-100K/year portfolio-tracking SaaS tool (Addepar) — complete with an embedded AI assistant that queries the underlying data directly. And on the personal side, an always-on iPhone recording app (built in 24 hours by the same friend who built Harbor) feeds a full day's audio transcript into GBrain, giving his agents ambient context on his actual day rather than only what he explicitly tells them — including a health-tracking use case where correlating years of Apple Watch data surfaced that his chronic nerve-pain flares are preceded by a wrist-temperature change three days in advance.

The GBrain-across-family-office/portfolio-companies pattern is a strong direct analogue for bi-data's own ambition to build a living context library over the BI function's institutional knowledge, and the "ask me questions to build your own prompt" technique is immediately teachable, no-jargon material for the 'Beyond Chat' course.
