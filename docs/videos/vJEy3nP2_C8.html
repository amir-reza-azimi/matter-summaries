Most Valuable Skill of 2026: Managing AI Agents

Channel: Greg Isenberg · Published: 2026-07-25 · Length: 45 min · Watch on YouTube

TL;DR: Ryan Carson, a 25-year founder now running his fourth company essentially solo with a seed round and fast-growing revenue, argues that whether you're technical or not, your job now is becoming a manager of agents. He walks through his actual physical setup, the mental shift required, and the concrete habits that let him ship 20 to 40 pull requests a day, often from his phone.

The physical setup. Ryan works from an eight-monitor-equivalent layout on one giant screen: Slack in a corner purely for agent notifications, a One Password vault always open, two windows for testing his live app, X in the corners, and Codex on the right for local work. The One Password habit is deliberate and important: he never gives an agent production write keys directly. When an agent needs to write to production, it has to ask him, and he manually copies the key into the session, so there's always a conscious human checkpoint before anything dangerous happens. For building, he uses Devon, from Cognition, alongside cloud agents generally, calling it one of the best 'software factories' in the industry, though not cheap.

Why cloud beats local. Devon's predecessor pitch two and a half years ago, an AI software engineer, mostly failed because the underlying models weren't good enough yet. In 2026, paired with a cloud-based harness, it works. The core argument is that working locally caps how many things you can do at once, because two pieces of work on your laptop risk colliding, forcing you into git worktrees or duplicate folders just to run two tasks in parallel. Cloud agents solve this by spinning up an isolated virtual machine per task: click a button, get a fresh disposable environment, and there's no collision risk no matter how many you run at once. Ryan runs five to ten cloud agents simultaneously this way. His blunt framing: 'if you are working locally, I honestly think you are a caveman... you are shipping ten times less than you could be.' He concedes local still makes sense for heavy front-end wireframing, but says you should move to cloud the moment you can.

Becoming more technical, not less. A counterintuitive point: Ryan says he is more technical now than at any point in his career, because managing agents well requires understanding Postgres, the difference between a dev and a production environment, and how migrations work, even if you never personally write the code. His analogy is engineering management: to be a good manager of many agents, you need real technical literacy, the same way a good carpenter is a much better manager of robot carpenters than someone who's never held a saw.

The new job is making high-stakes decisions all day, not writing code. Ryan reframes the actual work: where founders used to make two or three high-stakes calls a day, agent-managers now make ten to twenty by lunchtime. His system for not burning out: pin the threads representing the day's genuinely important work, let minor bug-fix threads sit unpinned until there's time, and settle into a roughly 25-minute check-in cadence rather than compulsively refreshing every thread all day. He shows his actual pull-request history averaging 22 to 25 merged PRs a day, sometimes 40, including a day he went hiking with his son and still shipped eight PRs first thing that morning from his phone, because agents don't stop working just because you're on a mountain.

Standout moment: asked why founders need to be reachable constantly now, Ryan's answer: 'why wait until you're in front of your Mac to give feedback... you have a team now that is infinitely scalable, they're going to do work very fast, and they're going to be blocked by you making important decisions... you can't delegate the decisions.' His related trick for managing model cost at scale: run one expensive frontier model (Fable) as a 'parent' thread that spins up cheaper child sessions to actually execute the work, rather than running the premium model everywhere.

Automations as the second unlock. Beyond running agents live, Ryan builds standing automations for anything he'd otherwise check on in a recurring meeting, his example being an automated browser test that signs up a fake client, creates a case, and walks through the full onboarding flow on a schedule, essentially continuous user-acceptance testing running unattended in the background.

Why this matters for my work: the credential-hygiene pattern (agents ask, a human pastes the key from a vault, nothing sensitive lives directly in an agent's context) is a concrete safety practice worth adopting for any client-facing agent work at Ranger AI, and the pin-and-cadence discipline for managing many concurrent Claude sessions is directly usable for Amir's own multi-agent BI and consulting workload. The 'expensive parent model spins up cheap child sessions' cost-control pattern is also worth testing against real BI/agent workflows where a premium model is currently running on every task by default.
