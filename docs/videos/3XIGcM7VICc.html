Learn These 6 AI Skills Now (Before Everyone Else Does)

Channel: Nate Herk · Published: 2026-06-15 · Length: 20 min · Watch on YouTube

This video lays out six skills meant to future-proof a career against AI-driven disruption, each paired with a specific, doable exercise rather than staying abstract, and is framed as applying regardless of job title or industry, since the argument is that AI will touch every role, not just technical ones.

Skill one is becoming "the AI person," which is explicitly relative: it doesn't mean being the best AI engineer alive, it means knowing more than the people immediately around you, which is achievable by anyone willing to experiment. The mechanism described is social and organic: people pick up AI almost like a hobby, build small things, automate a piece of their own job, then simply show someone, and that's usually all it takes for them to become known internally as the AI person, which matters because when a company has an AI moment (new model access, a new internal tool, a task force forming), someone in the room needs to already have a name to suggest. An IBM 2026 CEO study is cited finding 85% of CEOs expect every functional leader, not just technical ones, to become a technology expert in their own domain. The concrete exercise: pick one main AI tool, use it to deliver real, measurable ROI on one existing weekly task, and document exactly what changed, how long it took before versus after, and what still needed human judgment.

Skill two is taste and judgment, framed as the skill most people will get wrong precisely because AI output keeps getting more trustworthy-looking, which makes it easier to stop actually reading it. The suggested build-up: study the best existing work in your specific field, build a saved library of examples you actually like, and critically, whenever something AI produces is good, ask it why, what made it clear, what made it trustworthy, rather than just copying it forward silently. Every correction should be fed back explicitly, telling the model exactly what you changed and why, so the instructions the model works from actually improve over time rather than repeating the same error.

Skill three is context engineering, explicitly framed as succeeding prompt engineering: prompting is how you ask, context is what the model actually knows, and context is more durable because model quality keeps improving regardless of prompt cleverness, but no model improvement substitutes for it knowing what's actually in your business. Andrej Karpathy is cited describing context engineering as "the delicate art and science of filling the context window with just the right information." The concrete move: stop opening a blank chat every time; spin up a dedicated project and load in real documents (product details, a marketing calendar, past copy that worked, past copy that flopped) so the model works from your actual data instead of generic best practice, exactly like onboarding a new employee with real context rather than expecting them to guess.

Skill four is iteration speed, treated as inseparable from skill two but standing on its own because in an AI-native world the people who iterate fastest simply outperform everyone else, since every iteration is more data about what's working. The concrete moves suggested: master keyboard shortcuts and reduce mouse dependence, use voice input instead of typing where possible, and rapid-prototype an intentionally rough first version rather than trying to plan a perfect one. Just as important, and less discussed, is knowing when to stop iterating: tie every automation to one specific business metric defined before you start building (tickets resolved per day, qualified appointments per week, refund percentage reduced by X), build until you hit it, then move to maintenance rather than endlessly polishing.

Skill five is building your own "Jarvis," an always-on system that notices things and acts without you having to be the one triggering it every time, distinct from context engineering (teaching the AI what you know) because this is teaching it to act on that knowledge without you as the bottleneck. The suggested exercise is auditing your week for predictable triggers (a specific type of email arriving, every Monday morning, a new lead landing in a CRM) and, for each one, asking two questions: do I actually need to be the trigger, and does this step actually need AI at all, or would a simple deterministic workflow (the "vending machine" analogy: same input, same output, every time) do it more cheaply and reliably than an AI agent (the "slot machine": variable, powerful for genuine reasoning, but costlier and more failure-prone). Correctly choosing workflow-over-agent when a task doesn't need judgment is presented as the more advanced, higher-signal skill than defaulting to an agent for everything.

Skill six, income-stream diversification through AI, is framed less as "quit your job" and more as one core area of expertise expressed through several formats (a day job as the foundation, plus a course, a niche newsletter, consulting, or a micro-SaaS built off the same underlying expertise), explicitly warning against chasing unrelated income streams purely because they seem lucrative, since audiences and clients can tell, and burnout from spreading across disconnected domains is the more common failure mode than picking the wrong single niche.

Standout moments

The "vending machine versus slot machine" framing for deciding workflow-versus-agent is an unusually clean, immediately teachable heuristic that reframes agent-first thinking as often the less sophisticated choice, not the more advanced one. The IBM CEO statistic (85% expecting every functional leader to become a domain-level technology expert) is a concrete, citable data point for making the "become the AI person" argument to a skeptical colleague or client.

Why this matters for my work

The six-skill structure, especially context engineering over prompting and the vending-machine-versus-slot-machine decision test, is directly usable course material for teaching non-technical professionals to work with AI tools rather than defaulting to building an agent for everything. So I could adapt this six-skill framework as a lesson module structure for the Beyond Chat course, using the vending-machine-vs-slot-machine test as a standalone teachable exercise.
