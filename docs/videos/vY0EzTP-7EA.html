How I'd Make Money with Claude if my life depended on it

Channel: Nate Herk | AI Automation · Published: 2026-07-15 · Length: 9 min · Watch on YouTube

TL;DR: Nate argues the pure AI-agency gold rush is closing as more people crowd in, but a different and growing opportunity is becoming the embedded 'AI person,' either as a freelance consultant or, for most people, as an in-house consultant inside a company you already work for. He backs this with real labor-market data, then gives a concrete four-step roadmap for actually doing it.

The data behind the claim. McKinsey found 88% of companies now use AI somewhere in the business, but only about a third have scaled it past a few pilot projects, and just 6% see real measurable bottom-line impact. Accenture found nearly two-thirds of executives say their AI plans are stuck specifically because they lack in-house skills to execute them. ManpowerGroup, surveying 39,000 employers across 41 countries, found AI skills are now the hardest thing in the world to hire for, harder than traditional engineering roles. PwC's AI Jobs Barometer found workers with AI skills earn a 62% wage premium, and that premium keeps climbing every year it's been measured. LinkedIn's 2026 fastest-growing-jobs list ranks AI consultant and strategist second, behind only AI engineer.

Freelance versus in-house, and why in-house is the smarter default for most people. Freelance consulting offers real upside, freedom, no income ceiling, but comes with real churn risk and the grind of constantly finding and closing new clients. The in-house path trades some of that freedom for a stable paycheck plus a structural advantage no outside agency can match: you already know the business, the systems, and exactly where time is being wasted. Nate's core framing: 'become the AI person,' whichever path you pick, because this space is so new that a 15-year veteran and someone who graduated last month are starting from roughly the same line, and being early is what pulls the clients, promotions, and raises toward you later.

Step one: pick one painful problem and name a metric before building anything. List every manual task done last week, circle the one eating the most hours, then decide the specific number you're trying to move before touching any tooling, because that number, not the automation itself, is what you're actually selling. The three destinations worth selling are always time saved, mistakes cut, or money made. Nate's blunt framing: 'you're selling the destination, you're not selling the journey.'

Step two: build the fix and document it, without needing to code. For his own example, a Friday report built from scratch every week, this means feeding Claude past report examples, the template, and a checklist of where every number comes from, then iterating until it nails the report reliably every time. Then record a short before-and-after demo: what used to take four hours now takes ten minutes, run with one command.

Step three: deliver it and prove the exact metric moved. Show the four-hours-to-twenty-minutes result to whoever owns the problem, then ask one specific question afterward, 'does anyone have a task they'd want me to look at,' which Nate says does more work than any pitch, because every new task collected runs back through the same loop: find the constraint, name the metric, build the fix, prove it moved.

Step four: turn proven wins into money. For freelancers, the motion is warm outreach first, cold outreach for volume, then a specific pricing ladder: build for free once, sell paid audits, price actual builds off that audit, then convert to a monthly retainer to smooth out the income swings. For in-house operators, take the stack of proven wins to your manager, frame every win as your boss's win, and let the role formalize itself over time, since titles like 'AI enablement lead' are already appearing as job postings for exactly this function.

Standout moment: the reminder before step two even starts, 'only use AI tools that your company allows, and never put company or customer data into something that isn't approved,' a small but easy-to-skip governance point buried inside an otherwise purely tactical video.

Why this matters for my work: the four-step loop (pick a problem and name its metric before building, build and document without needing to code, prove the metric actually moved, then monetize through a free-build-to-audit-to-retainer ladder) is essentially a compressed version of what Ranger AI already does for clients, and is clean enough to lift directly into the course as a teachable framework for participants figuring out how to pitch their first AI win internally or externally.
