$30M Writer: Never write AI slop again

Channel: Greg Isenberg · Published: 2026-09-21 · Length: 95 min · Watch on YouTube

TL;DR

The argument is that AI makes generic output cheaper but does not make a distinctive point of view less valuable. The durable asset is a well-organised body of stories, opinions, methods, and lessons, called a digital brain in the conversation. It gives a writer or consultant material that only they can provide, while AI helps retrieve, adapt, and distribute it. The useful takeaway is to make original source material and a clear method the centre of a content system, rather than asking a model to manufacture authority.

AI raises the value of a point of view

The discussion pushes back on the claim that software and writing are automatically commoditised once models can generate them. A feature set can be copied. A writing cadence can be copied. What is harder to copy is a method that determines which problems are worth solving, which trade-offs matter, and how a person or company talks about them. The guest describes this as a product with a point of view: the product does not merely offer generic functionality, it helps users carry out a particular approach.

That distinction matters for content too. A model can write a competent post about AI adoption, but it cannot supply Amir's observed client patterns, hard-won operating choices, or personal judgments about which workflows actually help non-technical people. Those inputs are the source material. The model should make them easier to turn into different formats, not replace them with familiar language and broad claims.

Build a digital brain from durable inputs

A practical digital brain is not a giant uncurated archive. It is a retrievable collection of source material with enough context to reuse accurately. The conversation points to stories, opinions, lessons, and ideas. In practice, each item should retain where it came from, when it was true, the intended audience, the claim it supports, and any boundary on reuse. A note about a client pattern may be useful for a workshop, an essay, or sales copy, but only if it is separated from confidential detail and is not mistaken for a universal rule.

Start with the material that already has evidence behind it. A consulting engagement can produce a before-and-after workflow, a decision framework, an objection, a lesson, and a small example. A successful experiment can produce the setup, the observed result, the failure mode, and the next test. These are better building blocks than a pile of prompts. They provide both a reason to believe the content and a way to give it a distinctive voice.

Make the method visible in the product

One useful thread in the interview is that a method becomes a moat when it shapes ongoing product choices. If a product or service is built around a clear approach, new features and pieces of content have to fit that approach. That makes imitation harder because a competitor has to reconstruct the reasoning, not merely copy an output. The same is true of a personal brand. A memorable consulting proposition is not that an operator uses AI; it is the repeatable method through which teams discover high-value work, establish safe agent boundaries, and measure adoption.

This suggests a simple editorial test. Before publishing, ask whether a piece could plausibly have been written by anyone who followed the week’s AI news. If the answer is yes, it likely needs a real observation, an explicit decision rule, a counterexample, or a practical template. A point of view does not mean an unsupported hot take. It means taking a defensible position and showing the reasoning that makes it useful.

Use AI as a multiplier, not a substitute

The conversation does not require avoiding AI. It requires giving it better inputs. Once an original story or method is captured, a model can propose a headline, prepare alternate formats, find related past notes, tailor the language for a specific audience, or challenge an unclear claim. It can also flag when a draft has drifted into generic statements. Those are leverage tasks. The source still needs a human owner who decides what is true, what is sensitive, and whether the result sounds like them.

A good workflow keeps the original record close to the derivative. Store the source excerpt, the summary of what it proves, the intended message, and the generated variants. When an idea performs well, record why. When it feels flat or attracts the wrong audience, record that too. Over time this turns content work from isolated drafting into an editorial feedback loop with a growing body of grounded material.

Why this matters for my work

For Amir's consultancy and course, this is a useful corrective to generic AI marketing. So I could convert real operating lessons into a small, structured library of stories and methods, then use AI to adapt them for a course, portfolio, or client conversation while retaining the evidence and point of view that make them credible.
