AI Agents are the new SaaS

Channel: Greg Isenberg · Published: 2026-07-01 · Length: 26 min · Watch on YouTube

The argument here is that building AI agents is the new SaaS era, but with a bigger total addressable market, because instead of selling software, you're selling labor, and labor is a multi-trillion-dollar category. The video lays out a seven-step playbook for actually building and selling one of these businesses, not just the trend commentary the title might suggest.

The core mental-model shift: a normal SaaS product says here's a tool your team could use; an agent-first product says here's a job your team no longer has to do by hand. Two concrete examples anchor this: an AI receptionist for restaurants that answers calls, handles guest questions, manages reservations, and routes VIPs; and an AI dispatcher for home-services businesses (plumbing, HVAC, pest control) that answers calls, texts, books jobs, and reschedules. The pattern to look for: I handle this one annoying job better than a junior employee, faster than an agency, and cheaper than adding headcount.

Step two is picking a workflow with a paycheck already attached, meaning someone is already paying a human, an agency, or a receptionist to do it. A good agent workflow has five traits: it happens constantly (hourly beats daily), it has a clear finish line (the job got booked, the ticket got categorized), it already touches existing software (Gmail, Shopify, HubSpot, Stripe) so an agent has tools and context to work with, the edge cases are annoying but learnable (too basic and simple automation already solves it; too much pure judgment and the first version breaks), and the buyer can feel the loss when it's missing (missed calls, slow replies, empty calendar slots). The concrete first move: pick one niche, write down twenty specific jobs people complain about, then score each on frequency, pain, clarity of "done," tool access, and who owns the budget.

Step three, which the video says most founders skip, is shadowing the actual human doing the job before writing a single prompt: watching ten to twenty real cases, having them narrate what makes a case easy versus weird, what they check before deciding, and where mistakes happen. The real workflow is always deeper than the surface question; a restaurant host answering "what time are you open" also knows which tables suit strollers, when the patio closes, and how to route a VIP. Once you understand that detail, an agent spec needs seven parts: what wakes it up, what context it needs, what tools it can use, what it's allowed to do itself, where it needs approval, when it should escalate to a human, and what success looks like.

Step four is building the smallest useful agent rather than a fully autonomous employee, which is what breaks in most demos. Four good first versions, in ascending order of autonomy: draft-and-approve (reads context, drafts a reply or quote, a human approves), triage (classifies inbound work and routes it), coordinator (checks availability, sends reminders, keeps work moving between systems and people), and bounded-action (does one specific thing under clear rules, like processing a refund under $50). Anthropic's own guidance is cited here: many agent problems should start as workflows (predictable paths) and only earn dynamic, judgment-driven autonomy once that creates real value.

Step five is the product wrapper: what actually makes this a sellable SaaS product rather than a cool internal automation is that customers can see what happened, review logs and approvals, and test the agent before it goes live. This is where evals matter: take fifty real historical examples of the job, mark the correct answers, and run the agent against them, tracking exactly how many it classified correctly, how many it flagged for human review, and where it made mistakes. That transparency becomes a genuine sales asset. Step six is selling the pilot like labor first (a setup fee plus a simple monthly fee, moving toward outcome-based pricing once you understand what the customer actually values) and only productizing the repeated pattern once several clients in the same niche need the same thing. Step seven is distribution through "workflow teardowns," content that shows the old broken way (missed call, forgotten follow-up) against the new agent-handled way, because that contrast is what makes an owner feel the pain being solved.

Standout moments

"The product is the job" is the single sentence that reframes the entire pitch, and it's repeated deliberately enough that it should be treated as the organizing principle for any agent-business idea, not just a tagline. The concrete eval example, telling a property manager "we tested this on fifty of your old maintenance requests, it routed forty-two correctly, flagged six for review, made two mistakes, here's how we fixed them," is a genuinely reusable sales script.

Why this matters for my work

This is a directly usable structure for packaging AI consulting engagements as agent-first service offerings rather than generic "AI advice," with the five-trait workflow-selection checklist and the eval-as-sales-asset technique both immediately applicable to scoping a client pilot. So I could use the five-trait checklist to screen the next few client conversations for which workflow is actually agent-ready versus premature.
