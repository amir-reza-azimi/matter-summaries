I Studied 10,000 AI Websites… Here’s what I learned

Channel: Nate Herk | AI Automation · Published: 2026-11-09 · Length: 11 min · Watch on YouTube

TL;DR

Nate Herk argues that the difficult part of building a website with AI is no longer generating the first version. It is reviewing the result with enough marketing and design judgment to remove generic copy, weak hierarchy, decorative motion, and empty calls to action. His five-part rubric is practical: mirror the visitor's world, reserve contrast for the primary action, create visual depth, give motion a job, and let visitors sample the value before asking them to commit.

Make the visitor recognize themselves

The first test concerns copy rather than decoration. A strong first screen should tell visitors who the offer is for, what outcome they receive, and what to do next. Generic phrases such as “AI-powered scheduling” describe a feature but do not reflect a customer's situation. A line such as “fill your calendar without answering direct messages” names the outcome and the friction it removes.

The recommendation is to answer those three questions before prompting a model to design anything. That gives the model a concrete person, pain, and promise. The cited landing-page examples reinforce the idea that small changes to audience language can alter behavior, but the broader lesson is independent of any single conversion statistic: a page should sound as if it understands the visitor before it asks for attention.

Use contrast as an instruction

AI-generated sites often spread an accent color across headlines, icons, borders, backgrounds, and buttons. When everything is highlighted, nothing is. The proposed remedy is to use one contrasting color primarily for the action that matters. The familiar sixty-thirty-ten rule provides a simple constraint: most of the page uses a base color, a smaller share defines structure, and the smallest share is reserved for links or calls to action.

This turns color into navigation rather than decoration. It also gives an agent a rule that can be checked. Instead of asking for a premium visual style, the instruction can name the palette and specify exactly where the accent is allowed.

Build front, middle, and back

Flat collections of equally weighted cards tend to look cheap because the eye cannot tell what comes first. Herk uses a stage metaphor. The background establishes the scene, the middle contains the information, and the foreground holds the action. A photograph, shape, or color field can sit at the back; headlines and cards occupy the middle; the primary button or result is lifted into the foreground.

Depth does not require complex graphics. One element can break a section boundary, and a soft shadow can elevate the item that deserves attention. The important part is hierarchy: the visual layers should support the same sequence as the message.

Make motion earn its place

Animation can make a page feel polished or instantly reveal that it was generated without restraint. The rule is that every movement must do one job: demonstrate the product, guide attention to the next step, or show that an action has completed. If it has no job, remove it. If several elements compete for movement, choose one.

Headlines, body copy, and calls to action should be readable immediately rather than flying into place. Motion should also respect reduced-motion preferences, both for accessibility and comfort. This is a useful review standard because it connects animation to function and gives the user a way to opt out.

Offer a sample of the outcome

The final pattern replaces a generic contact form with a small piece of immediate value. A quiz can recommend the right service, an estimator can answer a pricing question, and a lightweight tool can help visitors understand their own situation. The best sample addresses a question customers repeatedly ask before buying.

The interaction should be short enough to finish and specific enough to feel personal. A visitor who contributes their own information receives a result that feels more relevant than a static claim. The page begins demonstrating competence before requesting a call, email address, or purchase.

A review rubric, not a generation trick

The five patterns work best as a checklist after the first AI-generated draft. Does the first screen identify the person, problem, promise, and next action? Is contrast reserved for what matters? Can the eye distinguish background, content, and action? Does every animation have a purpose and an accessible fallback? Is there a small useful result the visitor can experience immediately?

Those questions turn vague feedback such as “make it less AI-looking” into inspectable constraints. The model can still produce most of the implementation, but the human reviewer supplies the commercial intent and decides whether the page guides a real customer toward a real outcome.
