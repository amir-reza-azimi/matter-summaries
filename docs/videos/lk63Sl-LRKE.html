I let Codex use my computer. Here's what it can actually do.

Channel: How I AI · Published: 2026-07-11 · Length: 28 min · Watch on YouTube

TL;DR: Claire Vo demos browser and computer use, letting an AI literally control the mouse and keyboard on her behalf, across four concrete workflows: exhaustive automated QA testing, multi-persona product feedback, LinkedIn message triage, and a genuinely funny hands-free online shopping run. The throughline is that this stopped being a novelty and became something she uses 'every minute of every day.'

Setup, in one paragraph. Browser use needs the relevant desktop app (Codex or Claude) installed plus, if you want it to drive an actual browser tab rather than its own sandboxed window, the official browser extension installed in Chrome. Once both are in place, invoking it is as simple as typing '@browser' (a sandboxed browser window inside the app), '@Chrome' (drives your real logged-in Chrome via the extension), or '@computer' (full computer control, mouse and keyboard).

Use case one: exhaustive automated QA. On her own product's onboarding flow, she asked the agent to 'test the open onboarding flow for usability and mobile responsiveness, screenshot as you go, and make a spreadsheet of any issues.' It methodically tested every form field, dropdown, and viewport size, desktop and mobile, catching a genuine blocking bug she'd never noticed because she always tested the happy path herself: a required field with no validation, meaning users could click continue with nothing selected and simply get stuck. It came back with 11 issues logged, one high-severity, each with a screenshot, reproduction steps, and a fix, all placed directly into a tracked spreadsheet. Her tactical tip here: newer frontier models actually perform better underprompted. Telling it simply 'QA the onboarding flow' beat handing it a rigid 25-item checklist, because the model's own judgment about what to test was more thorough than her manually enumerated list.

Use case two: multi-persona critique, borrowed from her husband. Rather than one QA pass, she asked the agent to move through her product's core flow as three separate personas in sequence, a product manager quickly drafting a PRD after a customer meeting, an engineer picking up that PRD to write a technical spec, and a team lead trying to understand how their team actually uses the tool, then compile a research-style critique of friction points, delight moments, and improvement ideas across all three. The persona handoff surfaced a real structural gap that a single-persona test would have missed entirely: the 'engineer' persona expected to be able to reference an existing document from a different conversation thread and discovered it couldn't, exposing an actual product limitation the agent reasoned its way into by inhabiting a different user's mental model.

Use case three: LinkedIn message triage. With browser use logged into her own LinkedIn sidebar, she has it work through her unread messages, replying directly to routine friendly connection requests, and leaving her notes on anything that looks genuinely important or time-sensitive rather than replying on her behalf. Since LinkedIn has no official API or MCP integration, this is purely UI automation, and it turns an unmanageable backlog of hundreds of connection messages into a quick review pass. She also notes she'd been over-provisioning intelligence for this task (running a high-effort frontier model for what is fundamentally a simple triage job) and could likely get equivalent results faster on a cheaper, lower-effort model.

Use case four: hands-free personal shopping, with a caveat. Using '@Chrome' directly, she asked the agent to browse a clothing sale, pick ten items in her size that fit specific personal constraints (comfortable, breastfeeding-friendly, right for Hawaii's climate, as a mom six months postpartum), and add them to her cart without checking out, all running in the background while she did something else. The result wasn't perfect (it picked a mini skirt that didn't actually fit her stated constraints), a useful reminder that personal, nuanced constraints are exactly where computer-use agents still need a human to review before anything gets bought.

Why this matters for my work: the exhaustive-QA-into-tracked-spreadsheet pattern is directly reusable for testing this vault's own skills and scheduled tasks before they ship, and the underprompting tip (trust the frontier model's own judgment over a rigid checklist) is worth testing the next time a skill's instructions feel over-specified. The multi-persona critique technique is a genuinely new idea worth adapting for auditing Amir's own tools (the course platform, Ranger AI client deliverables) by having an agent walk through them as different named user types and compare what breaks for each.
