GPT 5.6 SOL IS HERE! How to use it.

Channel: Greg Isenberg · Published: 2026-07-03 · Length: 49 min · Watch on YouTube

Dan Shipper (Every, and the ChatPRD/Kora products) argues Codex with GPT-5.6 has become his genuine operating system for knowledge work, not just a coding tool — and walks through exactly how, live, rather than describing it abstractly. He frames GPT-5.6 as different in kind from Fable: Fable is described as a tactical, expert-level tool that requires real skill to wield well, while 5.6 is more like a well-rounded, easy-to-drive daily model that performs well across nearly everything asked of it without special handling.

The first concrete build is an email app (built from scratch and demoed live, eventually released open source) that sweeps every unread email into individual cards, each with a summary and a drafted reply already written in his voice — close enough, on routine scheduling-style emails, that he estimates 90% of drafts need no edits at all. The same card-plus-draft pattern extends beyond email into what he calls "feeds": a pinned thread per area of his life (a company feed, for instance) that sweeps unseen Slack messages and meeting notes into the same card format, learning over time which issues he actually cares about being surfaced and which can simply be archived, continuously revising its own prompts based on what gets kept versus dismissed.

A genuine "aha" moment came from referencing one Codex thread from within another — at that point the tool stopped feeling like a chatbot and started feeling like an actual operating system, because agents could hand context to each other rather than every thread starting cold. That's reinforced by an in-app browser used to build and test his own tools directly inside Codex (a markdown editor called Proof is the running example), letting him plan a day's agenda in one pane while the agent researches and drafts in another, all inside the same session.

Two more concrete patterns worth borrowing directly: a "mailroom" setup gives Codex its own email address using the Gmail plus-addressing trick (a random string appended to his own address), which an internal Slack agent can email whenever it needs something handled, instead of pinging Dan directly — with a router thread checking that inbox every five to fifteen minutes and dispatching each message to the right pinned feed. And a genuinely new capability in 5.6 called "goal," used for long, autonomous, hours-long runs with a clear completion condition — one example mentioned is a machine-learning fine-tuning job for automating copy edits, left running for over 20 hours unattended.

A live build demonstrates the loop directly: prompted to build a "Turnaround" SaaS product (a badge that displays how actively a piece of software is being maintained, addressing the fact that anyone can now one-shot a first version but ongoing maintenance is the actual differentiator), Codex's compound-engineering plugin runs a full plan-execute-review-compound loop, complete with a design checkpoint that asks clarifying visual-direction questions before continuing rather than guessing — reaching roughly 70% of a usable result before Dan notes he'd likely hand the remaining polish to Claude or Opus specifically for its stronger design sense, describing himself as a "pirate" who explores quickly and pairs with an "architect" (human or model) who turns a rough 70% into something genuinely polished.

The broader argument for software strategy: apps built specifically to be used inside an agent's in-context browser (rather than accessed the old way, switched away from) are an underrated opportunity, because most existing SaaS wasn't designed for an agent and a human to work the same surface together — and he expects app-store-style discovery for this category to emerge from the major labs within the year, the same way mobile app stores emerged once the smartphone platform matured.

The concrete, immediately transferable pieces for Amir are the mailroom pattern (giving an agent its own routable email address) and the pinned-feed structure (per-project or per-domain threads that continuously sweep and self-revise their own filtering prompts) — both are directly adaptable to the vault's existing memory/inbox-processing setup, and the pirate/architect framing is a useful lens for how Amir might pair fast exploratory building with more deliberate polish passes on client or course material.
