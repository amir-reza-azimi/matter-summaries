How the Morning Brew founder built a Claude content machine | Alex Lieberman

Channel: How I AI · Published: 2026-07-04 · Length: 43 min · Watch on YouTube

TL;DR: Alex Lieberman, Morning Brew co-founder now running 10X, built a genuinely sophisticated content pipeline he calls the content machine, anchored by a component called the Oracle, designed to solve two problems at once: he's capped at spending only about 25% of his time on content, and he wants every employee at his company to be able to create content around their real expertise without it reading as AI slop. The system is process-mapped, not just prompted, and includes a self-improving feedback loop.

The method before the machine: workflow-first mapping. Before building anything, Alex drew out his actual content process as it existed: find an idea, research it if it's not already lived experience, brain-dump thoughts, pick an anchor format, write, edit, revise, publish, then chop the anchor into micro-content for distribution. His explicit advice, which Claire calls 'the exact process I tell everybody to go through,' is to map the workflow first, decide where AI should be driver versus co-pilot at each step, and critically, map the workflow you'd run with no constraints at all rather than your current constrained version, because most of the efficiency gain comes from redesigning the process itself, not just automating the old one. He notes that doing this process-mapping exercise alone, before any AI is even involved, routinely surfaces waste most people never noticed in their existing workflow.

Step one, the Oracle. A directory of skills connected to all of 10X's systems of record (Slack, Notion, meeting notes, Linear, Git, Gmail), the Oracle scans the last seven days across every connected channel and surfaces roughly fifteen ranked 'content spikes' a day, using a real scoring rubric: a strong anecdote scores well, a strong point of view scores well, specific concrete examples score well. Half the spikes come from internal systems; the other half come from an 'internet reader' that watches a curated list of accounts across X and LinkedIn and surfaces good moments to quote-tweet or respond to. Alex calls this step alone possibly the most valuable part of the entire system, because even divorced from AI drafting anything, collapsing blank-page paralysis into a short list of concrete, scored ideas is what actually builds a consistent creation habit.

Step two, the interview panel. Once an idea is picked, six codified interviewer personas (Tim Ferriss, Joe Rogan, Michael Barbaro, Barbara Walters, Howard Stern, and others) ask questions one by one until they judge they've extracted enough material, with Alex answering by voice-to-text. If the topic needs outside information he doesn't already have, a separate research-assistant step builds a full brief first.

Step three, voice-matched drafting with a self-improving memory. Each team member keeps a personal folder holding their own codified voice: a style guide (role, owned assets, anchor content types, accounts they follow) and a voice guide built by studying their actual top-performing posts, down to hook formulas, structural patterns, and even a stated 'number one rule' (in Alex's case, write like you're texting a friend). A third file, content lessons, logs every piece of feedback Alex has ever given on tone or structure being wrong, and every future draft is checked against it before being shown to him. After he approves a final piece, a 'lessons loop' automatically diffs the original draft against the approved final version, extracts abstractable lessons, and asks him whether to add them to the permanent lessons file, so the system's model of his voice keeps tightening with every real piece of feedback rather than staying static.

Step four, the writers' council. A second panel of six codified writer personas (David Perell, Sean Puri, Morgan Housel, and others, including one he calls 'the AI slop allergist') each score the draft 1 to 10. If the aggregate score is under 9, the system runs a revision loop automatically until it clears that bar. Only then is the piece considered done.

Step five, repurpose and distribute. The finished anchor piece can be turned into other formats on request (three short tweets, two long LinkedIn posts), each written by studying how Alex has actually written in that specific format historically, rather than generically restyling the same text.

Standout moment: asked directly how he avoids AI slop, Alex's answer reframes the whole complaint: 'the only time the content machine actually produces slop is more of an indictment of the person not sharing good enough ideas during the interview step than the AI writing bad stuff... AI slop is hilariously people just pointing the finger at themselves.'

Why this matters for my work: this is close to a direct blueprint for a Pursuit and marketing content pipeline, an Oracle-style weekly scan across Amir's own sources for scored content spikes, a codified voice guide built from his actual best Pursuit issues and LinkedIn posts (the existing voice-sample.md is a natural seed for this), a lessons file that tightens with every edit he makes, and a scoring council before anything is called finished. The workflow-first, no-constraints mapping method is also directly transferable to any process Amir wants to redesign with AI at join.com or in Ranger AI engagements, not just content.
