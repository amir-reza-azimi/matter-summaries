5 Claude Cowork Scheduled Tasks (For Business Owners With No Time)

Channel: Simon Scrapes · Published: 2026-07-24 · Length: 15 min · Watch on YouTube

TL;DR: Simon walks through five scheduled tasks he actually runs inside Claude Cowork, all built on what Anthropic calls 'loop engineering': giving Claude a clear done condition and letting it cycle through work autonomously until that condition is met, instead of you prompting it turn by turn. Each task took him ten to fifteen minutes to set up and now runs unattended on a schedule.

The core idea: loop engineering. A loop is Claude repeating cycles of work until a done condition is satisfied. Instead of chatting back and forth, you define an outcome like 'keep going until every receipt is matched or flagged for review,' and Claude plans its own path to that outcome, only coming back to you if it genuinely needs input. Loops can trigger three ways: turn-based, where you still have to send a message, which defeats the point; time-based, on a schedule like every Friday at 4pm, which is what most of these five tasks use; and event-based, where something in the real world, like a meeting ending or an email arriving, kicks the loop off. The skill to learn, Simon says, is writing a tight, unambiguous done criteria, because the clearer that finish line, the faster the task actually works.

Task one is invoice and receipt reconciliation. Simon had over 600 unreconciled transactions sitting in his accounting software, Free Agent, because receipts land across three different Gmail inboxes and nobody had matched them up in months. The task runs every Friday at 4pm: it pulls the week's unexplained transactions, hunts through all three inboxes for matching receipts, attaches whatever it finds, and puts anything it can't match onto a gaps list that gets emailed to him. The done criteria is simple: every transaction from the week is either matched or logged as a gap. The technical snag was that Claude Cowork's built-in Gmail connector only reaches one account, so he routed around it using Composio, a connector layer with a generous free tier that lets Claude reach multiple Gmail accounts without ever seeing the actual credentials.

Task two is a subscription-creep audit. It pulls eight months of recurring bills, expenses, and subscription-like transactions, then compares this month against last month looking for price increases, duplicate accounts, and subscriptions that have gone quietly unused for six months. In its first real run, it caught that his recurring spend had risen fifteen percent month over month, mostly from Meta ads, but it also flagged four subscriptions that had crept up in price and three separate AI platforms running in parallel that he'd forgotten he was paying for on top of separate API usage. He gets the audit as an email with total spend versus last month, and it took about ten minutes to set up.

Task three is continuous email monitoring for voice-matched drafting. This one first builds a profile of how Simon actually writes email, reading ninety days of sent mail to extract his tone, greetings, and sign-offs, then pulls out his most commonly repeated email types (refund replies, cancellation responses, invoice chases) and turns each into a template. Because scheduled tasks in the cloud can't reach local files, he stores all of this context in Notion instead of a local folder, which also happens to make it shareable across a team. Once that voice profile and template library exist, a scheduled task can check his inbox and draft replies in his actual voice, in his actual style, without him touching it.

Task four turns meeting recordings into a live to-do list. Simon uses Fathom for meeting notes but almost never goes back and reviews the recordings or actions afterward. So twice a day, at 11:30am and 4:30pm on weekdays, a task pulls his Fathom action items and drops them directly into his Notion task list with the due date, who it's assigned to, and a link back to the exact moment in the recording where it was said. He hasn't yet taught it to auto-assign tasks to specific teammates, but the logging alone means he's no longer manually retrieving action items from a tool he never opens.

Task five, the one he says he'd keep if he could only keep one, is a weekly competitor watch. For his second business, Rankspot AI, he tracks 30 to 50 competitors, and manually watching all their YouTube, LinkedIn, X, blog, and product updates is impossible. The task visits every competitor's website and socials weekly, scrapes what changed, and sends a ranked digest of the most active competitors with sourced links. In its very first run it found that 14 of 55 tracked competitors had made moves that week, and named the top mover by name along with exactly what they published and when, so Simon could decide whether to copy the move. The done criteria: check every competitor on the list and report on each one, whether they moved or not.

Why this matters for my work: this is close to a direct template for the personal-operations stack Amir is already running, the scheduled-task pattern behind things like this very video digest and package tracking. The Composio workaround for multi-account connector limits, and storing shared context in Notion instead of local files because cloud-run tasks can't reach a laptop's file system, are two concrete technical constraints worth remembering before building the next scheduled task. The weekly competitor-watch and subscription-audit patterns are also directly reusable for BI and consulting client work, so this could become a template Amir hands to consulting clients: pick one recurring, well-defined business chore, write a tight done criteria, and ship it as a scheduled task in an afternoon.
