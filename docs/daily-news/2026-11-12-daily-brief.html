Ukraine and Russia agree a large prisoner exchange, but talks produce no wider breakthrough

Russia and Ukraine agreed in principle to a large prisoner-of-war exchange after two days of US-mediated talks in Abu Dhabi. Both sides confirmed the plan, although final numbers still had to be settled and the wider negotiations did not bridge the central disagreements over occupied territory, postwar security guarantees or Europe’s role. US envoys were due to continue meetings with Vladimir Putin and Volodymyr Zelenskyy. The exchange is a meaningful humanitarian outcome for detainees and their families, but it is not a ceasefire. The talks therefore create a modest channel for further diplomacy while leaving the military conflict and the political settlement far from resolution.

COP31 leaders press for faster climate action after another year of extreme weather

World leaders opened the political phase of the COP31 climate talks in Turkey with calls for stronger and faster action, after a year marked by floods, heatwaves and other climate-related disasters. The conference now has to move from broad declarations to negotiated commitments on emissions, finance and implementation. The urgency is heightened by evidence that current national plans remain insufficient to meet the Paris Agreement’s temperature goals. What matters next is whether major emitting countries support language that translates concern into measurable policy and funding, particularly for countries facing the most acute climate damage and the hardest choices on energy transition.

Brazil’s electoral court fines Facebook over compliance during the presidential runoff

Brazil’s top electoral court fined Meta’s Facebook $1.3 million for failing fully to comply with judicial orders during the presidential runoff campaign. The court said Facebook had not adequately met demands connected to complaints that content involving the two candidates had been treated differently. It did not find evidence, at this stage, that the company intended to influence the outcome. The ruling matters because it shows how election authorities are testing the practical reach of platform obligations during campaigns. For Meta and other platforms, the next question is whether courts move from case-by-case enforcement toward more prescriptive rules on transparency, content treatment and response times.

Broader US inflation pressure raises the prospect of faster Federal Reserve rate hikes

Fresh US inflation data showed price growth spreading beyond the immediate shocks from tariffs, gas and heavy data-centre investment, according to the Associated Press. Consumer prices rose 4% year on year in October, up from 3.7% in September, while two Federal Reserve officials indicated that faster interest-rate increases may be needed. That would affect borrowing costs for mortgages, cars and credit cards, not just financial markets. The policy question is whether the increase proves persistent once energy pressures ease. Until that is clearer, the data make a quick return to easier monetary policy less likely and keep pressure on households already facing higher living costs.

Berlin launches campaign against violence and aggression toward municipal workers

Berlin launched its “More Respect. More Value for Berlin” campaign with 14 municipal companies, responding to rising reports of disrespectful behaviour and violence toward employees working in public-facing services. The organisations involved include BSR, Vivantes, public housing companies, utility operators and other city-linked employers. Governing Mayor Kai Wegner called for stronger protection, tougher penalties and better tracking of assaults, saying the current reporting system does not reach every state employee. The campaign itself is symbolic, but the practical test is whether it leads to shared incident data, better prevention and stronger support for staff whose jobs keep everyday city services operating.

Permit clears 500-apartment development on the long-empty site near Brandenburg Gate

Berlin’s Mitte district has granted a building permit for “The Wilhelm,” a 500-apartment and commercial development on Wilhelmstraße near Brandenburg Gate. The site has been an excavation pit since 2016 after the earlier project stalled, making the approval a visible step toward resolving a long-running gap in the city centre. The revised plan shifts away from large condominiums and penthouses toward standard-sized homes of different sizes. Completion is scheduled for 2028. The project will not solve Berlin’s housing shortage on its own, but it is a concrete test of whether stalled central sites can finally be converted into more broadly usable housing.

technology · notable

European Commission proposes Digital Fairness Act for online shopping and subscriptions

The European Commission proposed a Digital Fairness Act aimed at bringing online consumer protections closer to those that apply offline. The proposal targets dark patterns, hidden fees, misleading scarcity prompts, unclear influencer marketing, unfair personalisation involving minors and subscription traps. It would also require clearer pricing in virtual queues and make some digital cancellations easier. The Commission says the rules could save consumers up to €13.7bn a year, although the proposal still needs the normal EU legislative process. For online businesses, the significance is less a single ban than a broader shift toward design and marketing practices being assessed as consumer-protection issues rather than merely product choices.

world · notable

EU and Nigeria sign readmission agreement on returns and migration cooperation

The European Union and Nigeria signed a readmission agreement intended to establish clearer procedures for identifying and returning people who no longer have a right to stay in either side’s territory. The agreement forms part of a wider EU-Nigeria partnership on migration, security, legal pathways and action against trafficking and smuggling. It is not yet in force: the European Parliament must give consent and the Council must complete ratification. The agreement matters because readmission arrangements are central to the EU’s migration policy but are politically sensitive and operationally difficult. Its effects will depend on implementation, safeguards and whether the wider partnership delivers credible cooperation beyond returns.

The European Commission and Ukrainian authorities are convening an EU-Ukraine Investment Conference in Warsaw to connect companies, investors, public authorities and financing institutions around reconstruction. The conference will show whether those elements are becoming concrete enough for private investment to scale.

The European Commission launched two regional security initiatives with Southern Mediterranean partners under its Pact for the Mediterranean. One is designed to deepen judicial and law-enforcement cooperation against organised crime, while the other focuses on cooperation against illicit trafficking across air, sea and land borders. Their value will depend on whether the structured dialogue produces operational cooperation rather than another forum. For Europe, the initiatives reflect the link between external partnerships, migration management, infrastructure security and the stability of trade routes across the Mediterranean.

EU defence-industry call attracts 58 proposals involving member states and Ukraine

The first EU call under the Ukraine Support Instrument for missiles, ammunition and bombs received 58 proposals involving applicants from 11 member states and Ukraine. The programme is aimed at expanding industrial capacity for air-defence missiles, ammunition, counter-drone systems, rockets, bombs and loitering munitions. The Commission says €184.5m is available, with up to €30m per project, and expects results in March 2027. The high participation signals strong demand for cross-border defence production partnerships, especially as Ukraine’s needs and European rearmament overlap. The decisive step now is evaluation: proposals do not create output until they are selected, contracted and converted into capacity on the ground.

ExxonMobil challenges EU carbon-capture rules in court

ExxonMobil has launched a legal challenge to European Union carbon-capture rules, the Financial Times reported. The case puts a major oil company’s investment and regulatory obligations against the EU’s effort to use carbon capture and storage as part of its industrial decarbonisation strategy. Legal challenges can slow implementation, clarify the limits of regulation or reshape compliance costs even when the underlying policy remains intact. The immediate question is the specific timing and obligations the company seeks to suspend. More broadly, the case will be watched as a test of how far European climate rules can require fossil-fuel producers to contribute to the infrastructure needed for emissions reduction.

German economic advisers urge Merz to curb defence borrowing

Germany’s Council of Economic Experts has urged Chancellor Friedrich Merz to bring defence spending gradually into the regular budget rather than rely heavily on borrowing, the Financial Times reported. The advisers warned that debt could reach 98% of gross domestic product by 2040 if the current approach is not adjusted. The argument reflects a wider European tension: governments want to rebuild military capability quickly while retaining fiscal credibility and room for other priorities. The advice is not government policy, but it adds weight to the debate over how Germany should finance rearmament. Future budget decisions will show whether ministers accept a tighter path or prioritise flexibility through additional borrowing.

Spain faces its first general strike in 14 years over housing costs and wages

Spain is experiencing its first general strike in 14 years as unions protest soaring housing costs and low wages ahead of the country’s November 29 snap election, the Financial Times reported. A general strike turns pressures that many households face individually into a direct political test for the government and opposition. Housing affordability has become a defining issue across several European cities, but nationwide labour action makes the economic and electoral stakes more immediate in Spain. The next question is whether disruption shifts the campaign agenda toward wages, rents and social policy, or whether it hardens existing political divisions without producing a durable response.
