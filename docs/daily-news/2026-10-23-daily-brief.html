Seven tankers are reported damaged in the Strait of Hormuz

Iranian state television said seven oil tankers caught fire or were damaged overnight in the Strait of Hormuz, either after Iranian attacks or naval mines. The AP could not independently confirm the claim, but it comes amid the heaviest reported month for commercial-shipping incidents since the conflict began. Tankers are still moving through a US-protected route close to Oman, sometimes with tracking systems switched off, while transfers farther out in the Gulf of Oman add time and cost. The immediate issue is not only physical security: higher charter rates and slower logistics can feed directly into energy prices and industrial supply chains.

Kyiv is hit as Russia and Ukraine signal talks could resume

Russia launched an hourslong ballistic-missile barrage on Kyiv while Moscow said it had intercepted more than 800 Ukrainian drones. Ukrainian officials reported at least four deaths and dozens of injuries. The strike came only hours after Vladimir Putin agreed in principle to resume US-mediated three-way talks with Russia and Ukraine, following meetings involving US envoy Steve Witkoff and President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. The juxtaposition matters because it tests whether the diplomatic channel represents an operational opening or merely runs alongside continuing escalation.

Ukraine’s military satellite access remains disputed

US officials said SpaceX’s military-grade Starshield service was again operating at full speed for Ukraine, but a European official and Ukrainian accounts said meaningful restrictions remained. Starshield and the civilian Starlink network support Ukrainian communications and drone operations, so the reported slowdown had immediate battlefield effects, including disrupted missions and lost drones. The competing claims leave the practical status unresolved rather than settled. The episode also exposes a broader dependency: a privately controlled communications system has become central to a state’s wartime capability, making technical policy decisions a consequential part of diplomacy and military planning.

Washington imposes travel bans on 61 Bolivian and Colombian officials

The US announced travel bans on 61 current and former officials, judges and lawmakers from Bolivia and Colombia over alleged corruption and narcotrafficking. The measures revoke existing visas where applicable and block future applications, according to the State Department. Forty of those named are Bolivians and 21 Colombians, spanning parliamentarians, former officials and local politicians. Such visa sanctions are narrower than broad economic sanctions, but they can reshape elite incentives and diplomatic relations by targeting personal mobility and access. The action signals that Washington is tying regional cooperation on trafficking and governance more directly to individual accountability.

US early voting passes 20 million ballots ahead of the midterms

Associated Press voting data showed more than 20 million Americans had cast early ballots less than two weeks before the Nov. 3 midterm election. The tally covers 46 states and is roughly one-third of the early-vote total reported ahead of the previous midterm cycle. Texas and California had the largest raw totals, while turnout was particularly strong in several states expected to decide control of the Senate. Early voting does not predict a winner by itself, but it changes campaign logistics and can reveal where parties have succeeded at mobilising supporters. It will be watched closely as a measure of whether cost-of-living and war concerns are translating into participation.

US voters put living costs and ending the Iran war ahead of investigations

A new AP-NORC poll found broad agreement among US registered voters that lowering the cost of living and ending the war with Iran should be top priorities for the next Congress. Roughly eight in ten respondents placed affordability near the top, and about seven in ten said the war should be a high priority. Far fewer prioritised investigating or advancing the president’s agenda. The finding is relevant beyond US politics because it links household inflation, energy risk and foreign policy in the same voter calculation. It also raises pressure on elected officials to show that economic policy can cushion external shocks rather than simply describe them.

investment · notable

AI shares fall sharply as oil risk adds to a global market reset

A broad selloff in AI-linked equities pulled the Nasdaq down about two-point-six percent and the S&P 500 down about one-point-two percent, even as most US stocks rose and the Dow was little changed. Investors focused on the cost of financing data centres and chips, as well as elevated valuations after a long AI-led rally. Nvidia fell four-point-four percent and debt-sensitive cloud provider CoreWeave dropped ten-point-one percent, while Asian technology-heavy indexes also declined. At the same time, Brent crude rose about four percent to a five-month high on concern about the Iran conflict. This is market context, not a trading signal: it shows two separate risk channels, capital costs and energy supply, moving together.

Falling yields may ease that pressure at the margin, but the underlying question remains whether projected revenues will justify the sector’s financing needs.

technology · watch

The Pentagon moves to bar personnel from using nonpublic information in prediction markets

The Pentagon issued a memo reiterating that military and civilian personnel may not use classified or nonpublic information to trade on prediction-market platforms. The policy reflects a concern that trading around military outcomes can expose operational signals even when individual trades appear small. The memo points to both administrative punishment and potential criminal consequences for violations. Prediction markets have expanded from a niche product into a more visible part of financial and political information systems, which makes the distinction between public analysis and inside knowledge increasingly consequential. This is a governance story as much as a market one: institutions are adapting old information-security rules to newer forms of financial activity.

The US State Department restores three foreign-service fellowship programmes

The US State Department said it will renew the Pickering, Rangel and Payne fellowships after earlier suspensions or cutbacks, with higher stipends and living-expense support but lower enrolment. The programmes are designed to create a route into the foreign service for graduate students, and their return follows criticism from members of Congress over reductions in the diplomatic corps. The practical scale and revised admission criteria are still unclear. The decision is notable because it reverses part of a wider retrenchment in diplomatic capacity at a time when the United States is managing simultaneous security, sanctions and alliance pressures. Implementation details will determine whether the restoration meaningfully rebuilds the pipeline.
