Riyadh airport attack kills 12 and injures hundreds

Saudi authorities said a Houthi attack on Riyadh airport killed 12 people and wounded more than 300, making it one of the most consequential recent strikes connected to the wider regional war. The attack disrupted airport operations and intensified concern that the conflict is extending further into Gulf infrastructure rather than remaining confined to maritime routes and battle zones.

The immediate issue is civilian protection and aviation continuity, but the broader consequence is strategic: attacks on a major Saudi hub increase pressure on regional governments and their allies to reassess air defence, transport resilience and escalation risks. The incident also adds another live variable for energy, travel and shipping markets already affected by the conflict.

Hurricane Isaias hits US energy operations

Hurricane Isaias made landfall in the United States, killing at least four people and leaving roughly 670,000 customers without power, according to Reuters. Beyond the immediate human impact, the storm disrupted oil and gas operations, showing how quickly weather events can become an energy-supply and industrial-continuity issue.

The relevant next stage is restoration: utilities, producers and ports will need to establish the scale and duration of outages, while insurers and local authorities assess damage. Because energy markets are already navigating high geopolitical risk, a weather-driven interruption can have an outsized effect on prices and logistics even if production losses prove temporary. This is context, not an investment signal.

Hurricane Simon strengthens off Mexico's Pacific coast

Hurricane Simon strengthened to Category 4 intensity off Mexico's Pacific coast, prompting preparations along a coastline exposed to strong winds, surf and potentially dangerous rainfall. The storm's rapid intensification makes it a separate operational concern from the concurrent US landfall of Isaias, rather than a repeat of that story.

Authorities and communities will focus on the forecast track, evacuation decisions and the reliability of transport and communications as the system approaches land. For a daily brief, the importance lies in the combination of immediate public-safety risk and pressure on regional infrastructure, tourism and ports. The situation remains forecast-dependent, so the key update to watch is whether Simon maintains intensity near populated coastal areas.

Infosys says US residency-rule pause will not materially hurt it

Infosys said it does not expect a material impact from the US government's suspension of Permanent Labor Certification, or PERM, applications. The announcement matters because the process is a core channel through which large employers sponsor workers for permanent residency, and technology and IT-services groups depend heavily on predictable immigration administration.

The company-specific assessment does not settle the wider question for the sector. Other employers may face different workforce mixes, timing exposures or hiring needs, while the policy could still alter future recruitment plans. The next useful evidence will be guidance from US authorities and comparable disclosures from affected companies, especially those with large cross-border engineering and consulting workforces.

Watch for concrete delivery terms and wholesale-price data rather than relying on the announcement alone.

The Financial Times reports that borrowing to fund AI infrastructure is slowing as investors become more cautious after months of heavy bond issuance. That matters because the data-centre build-out has relied not only on equity enthusiasm but also on debt markets willing to finance expensive, long-lived capacity before revenue is fully proven.

For the broader market, this is a useful stress test of whether the AI expansion can be funded on ordinary commercial terms rather than momentum alone. economy · notable

India's central bank moves to support the rupee

India's central bank is seeking to stabilise the rupee as it approaches record lows, the Financial Times reported. Rising energy costs associated with the Iran war are adding pressure to the currency of the world's fastest-growing major economy, placing external-price shocks alongside domestic growth and inflation considerations.

Currency weakness can make imports, especially fuel, more expensive and complicate the trade-offs faced by monetary authorities. The immediate focus will be whether intervention and policy signalling limit disorderly moves without undermining reserves or growth confidence. For companies with Indian supply chains or revenue exposure, the relevant impact is on input costs, imported equipment and cross-border pricing rather than a simple verdict on the economy's underlying momentum.

The European Union's proposed common budget has been reduced as negotiators seek an agreement before year end, according to the Financial Times. The revised plan may improve the chances of a deal, but it can also sharpen disagreements over who pays and which programmes are protected. The next meaningful development is the negotiating package, not the headline size alone.
