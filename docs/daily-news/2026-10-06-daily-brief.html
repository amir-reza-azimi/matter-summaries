Trump loosens red-diesel rules amid fuel-price pressure

President Donald Trump signed an order allowing wider tax-free use of red-dyed diesel, presenting it as a way to lower costs for truckers, farmers and ultimately consumers. The fuel is normally restricted to off-road uses because it is exempt from federal and state excise taxes, while the administration says the change responds to a sharp rise in diesel prices after the Iran war.

The policy is politically salient ahead of US midterm elections, but the practical effect is disputed. An industry analyst told AP that farmers already use red diesel, making supply and energy prices more important than the tax rule for the people the order is meant to help.

France’s central bank governor warned that the country risks being squeezed by interest costs as investors focus on its public finances. Germany and France agreed on backing a last-resort European Union instrument designed to deter trade threats, according to the Financial Times. The measure is described as a second-strike tool for use when a partner uses economic leverage over strategic supplies, including Chinese rare earths and magnets.

Berlin’s support is important because it points to greater willingness to use collective economic power after years of caution about escalation. The debate is not simply about tariffs: it is about how the EU protects supply chains and strategic industries while keeping open trading relationships with countries whose materials remain difficult to replace.

Euro falls to a 17-month low against the dollar

The euro fell to its weakest level against the dollar in 17 months as high energy costs and anxiety about France’s public finances weighed on the single currency. The move reflects a broader shift toward the dollar during a period of elevated yields and political uncertainty across several large economies.

For Europe, a weaker euro can raise the local-currency cost of imported energy and other dollar-priced goods, while helping exporters at the margin. The move is context rather than a trading signal, but it reinforces how fiscal credibility, energy exposure and interest-rate expectations are interacting in today’s markets.

Oil groups take a climate-damages fight to the US Supreme Court

ExxonMobil and Suncor Energy are asking the US Supreme Court to block state-level climate damages claims, putting a pivotal legal question before the country’s highest court. The companies are challenging whether states can use their own legal systems to seek compensation for climate-related harm from fossil-fuel producers.

The case could determine where and how climate accountability litigation proceeds in the United States. A ruling limiting state actions would narrow a route used by local governments to pursue damages, while a ruling allowing them to continue could increase legal exposure for oil and gas companies beyond federal environmental regulation.
