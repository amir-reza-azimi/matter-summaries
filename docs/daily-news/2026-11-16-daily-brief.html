Yemeni government forces retreat from a shipping chokepoint as Bahrain intercepts Iranian attacks

Saudi-backed forces in Yemen said they were withdrawing from the Bab el-Mandeb area to strengthen defenses around Aden, reversing gains from a six-week counteroffensive against the Houthis. The retreat leaves the rebels better positioned along the entrance to the Red Sea, while fighting around Taiz is worsening humanitarian conditions. AP reported that clashes were also obstructing assistance to thousands of refugees.

Separately, Bahrain said it intercepted Iranian aerial attacks before they reached their targets. Iran claimed a strike on an American base, but damage was not independently established in the reporting. The two developments put shipping routes and Gulf security under renewed pressure just as Washington has been discussing restraint rather than another major military campaign.

Estonia will close its Russian border after an alleged sabotage attempt

Estonia’s foreign minister said all border crossings with Russia would close from December 1 after authorities detained eight people over an attempted arson attack on drone manufacturer Threod Systems. Tallinn accused Russia’s military intelligence service of ordering the operation. Moscow has denied involvement in European sabotage cases, and its foreign ministry denounced the planned closure as hostility toward Russia.

The three remaining crossings handle pedestrians and freight, so the decision would turn a security investigation into a practical restriction on cross-border movement. No end date was specified. AP independently reported the closure and attempted attack, placing the development within growing concerns about threats to European defense manufacturing. The attribution remains Estonia’s allegation, rather than an established court finding.

Hundreds of thousands protest Czech plans to change public-media funding

Huge crowds gathered in Prague against a proposal to replace household broadcasting fees with state-budget funding and cut public broadcasters’ financing. Organizers estimated attendance at about 400,000, a figure attributed to them rather than an independently confirmed count. The bill has passed an initial parliamentary vote but still faces further debate, following an earlier strike by television and radio workers.

Critics argue that direct dependence on government budgets would weaken broadcasters’ independence and expose them to political pressure. Prime Minister Andrej Babis says the change fulfills a promise to reduce households’ financial burden, while his government accuses media outlets of bias. The dispute concerns both the funding level and whether the proposed structure adequately protects editorial decisions from political intervention.

Sweden’s Centre Party backs Andersson, but a government deal remains unfinished

Sweden’s Centre Party agreed to proceed with Social Democrat leader Magdalena Andersson’s proposal for a new government, offering a possible route out of the deadlock after September’s election. Andersson wants to govern with the Centre Party and Greens. Her bloc won a narrow parliamentary majority, but its parties have spent weeks unable to settle how power would be shared.

The remaining obstacle is the Left Party: its votes or tolerance would be needed, yet it insists on entering government, which the Centre Party rejects. Centre leader Elisabeth Thand Ringqvist said final details still needed negotiation. The endorsement advances coalition formation without guaranteeing that Andersson can survive a parliamentary vote; resolving the competing demands remains the decisive next step.

NATO will skip its planned leaders’ summit in Albania in 2027 and reduce other scheduled ministerial meetings, AP reports. Albanian Prime Minister Edi Rama said Tirana could host in 2028 if a summit takes place. Secretary General Mark Rutte said meetings would be held when needed.

The reorganization comes amid disagreements over defense spending, Iran and Ukraine. It reduces opportunities for coordination as allies continue debating their collective security and defense-spending commitments.

Moldova’s pro-Russian Gagauzia region heads for a leadership runoff

Alexandr Stoianoglo and Socialist Party candidate Fiodor Gagauz will contest a November 29 runoff to lead Gagauzia, Moldova’s autonomous southern region. With all votes counted, neither reached the majority required for an outright victory. The election replaces Evgenia Gutul, who was sentenced last year for illegally channeling Russian money into a political party; Moscow has condemned her imprisonment.

The regional contest sits within Moldova’s wider struggle over its European direction and relations with Russia. President Maia Sandu’s government seeks EU membership by 2030 and accuses Moscow of interference, which the Kremlin denies. Gagauzia’s electorate generally favors closer Russian ties. The runoff will determine its next regional leader while leaving those broader disputes over influence and autonomy unresolved.

economy · notable

Japan’s economy unexpectedly contracts as household spending weakens

Japan’s economy shrank at an annualized rate of one-tenth of a percent in the third quarter, against expectations for modest growth. Private consumption fell three-tenths of a percent as higher living costs squeezed households, while corporate capital spending rose strongly. Imports also increased, leaving net trade as a drag on overall output and producing the first contraction in four quarters.

The figures show an uneven recovery rather than weakness across every part of the economy. Economists quoted by Reuters said the result might not derail further Bank of Japan rate increases, because inflation remains persistent and business investment is resilient. Continued weakness in consumption could nevertheless strengthen arguments for caution. The policy question is how much additional tightening households can absorb.

Vulcan launch failure destroys 40 Amazon internet satellites

A United Launch Alliance Vulcan rocket failed roughly ten minutes after liftoff from Florida, destroying 40 satellites intended for Amazon’s Leo internet constellation. ULA said the mission did not reach orbit and that it had not yet determined the cause. The Boeing-Lockheed Martin venture had maintained a perfect mission-success record since its formation in 2006; this was Vulcan’s fifth flight.

The loss compounds Amazon’s difficulty securing reliable launch capacity for a network meant to compete with Starlink. Reuters reported that only about 12% of the satellites needed for global Leo coverage had been deployed before this mission. A technical investigation could keep Vulcan unavailable for months, creating a scheduling problem beyond the immediate loss of the spacecraft.

Johnson & Johnson could announce an acquisition of eye-disease developer Ollin Biosciences as soon as Monday, the Financial Times reported, according to Reuters. The immediate question is whether the companies confirm an agreement and its terms.

Intel shareholders sue over disclosures before a steep stock-price fall

Intel faces a proposed shareholder class action alleging that it concealed the effect of weak PC sales and excess inventory before an October share-price collapse. The lawsuit, filed Friday, names the company, Chief Executive Lip-Bu Tan and finance chief David Zinsner. Investors allege that management was slow to reveal pressures on revenue while raising substantial equity capital during the quarter; Intel had not responded to Reuters’ request for comment.

The allegations have not been established in court. The case concerns whether investors received adequate information before the company’s disappointing outlook and roughly 29% one-day decline. It also highlights the tension between Intel’s costly manufacturing expansion, supply constraints and continued exposure to the PC market despite growth in data-center demand.

investment · notable

Oil falls as recovering supply and weaker Chinese refining change the outlook

Brent crude traded near $95 a barrel early Monday as markets weighed recovering Gulf exports against weaker demand for crude. Chinese refinery throughput fell in October from both the previous month and a year earlier. The International Energy Agency now expects fourth-quarter supply to exceed demand by about 1,700,000 barrels per day, reversing its earlier forecast for a deficit.

That shift helps explain falling benchmark prices even while the Middle East remains dangerous. Reuters reported that tanker availability, freight costs and risks around Hormuz were still keeping shipping expensive. Market context therefore contains two different pressures: more available oil can ease crude prices, while insecure transport can keep delivery costs elevated. Neither amounts to a settled forecast for consumer fuel prices.
